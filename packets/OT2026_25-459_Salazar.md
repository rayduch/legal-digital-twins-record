## Question(s) presented (verbatim from the Court's QP list)
The Video Privacy Protection Act ("VPPA") contains a one-sentence liability clause. It prohibits a "video tape service provider" from "knowingly disclos[ing], to any person, personally identifiable information concerning any consumer of such provider." 18 U.S.C. § 2710(b)(1). The statute defines "consumer" broadly to include a "subscriber of goods or services from a video tape service provider." Id. § 2710(a)(1). It defines "personally identifiable information" to include information that "identifies a person as having requested or obtained specific video materials or services from a video tape service provider." Id. § 2710(a)(3). And it defines "video tape service provider" to include those in the business of delivering audiovisual materials. Id. § 2710(a)(4).

Paramount is a "video tape service provider." Both courts below assumed as much. Michael Salazar subscribed to Paramount's online newsletter, which he used to view videos. Paramount then disclosed Mr. Salazar's Facebook ID and his video-watching history to Facebook. That information counts as "personally identifiable information." Again, both courts below assumed as much.

The question here is whether the phrase "goods or services from a video tape service provider," as used in the VPPA's definition of "consumer," refers to all of a video tape service provider's goods or services or only to its audiovisual goods or services.

## Lower court and holding (two sentences)
United States Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit, 133 F.4th 642 (divided panel; Bloomekatz, J., dissenting). Held: "goods or services" in §2710(a)(1) is limited to audiovisual goods or services, so a person who subscribed only to a website's email newsletter, which is not audiovisual material, is not a "consumer" and cannot sue under the VPPA, affirming the district court's dismissal with prejudice.

## Procedural posture
Petitioner signed up for the 247Sports.com email newsletter (247Sports is owned by Paramount) with his email address, and alleges that while logged into Facebook he watched videos on the site and that Paramount's tracking pixel sent his Facebook ID and video-viewing history to Facebook for targeted advertising without his consent. The district court (M.D. Tenn.) dismissed the VPPA claim with prejudice for failure to allege that he was a "consumer"; the Sixth Circuit affirmed 2–1 and denied rehearing en banc. The Second Circuit (Salazar v. National Basketball Association, 2024) and the Seventh Circuit (Gardner v. Me-TV, 2025) read the definition the other way; the D.C. Circuit (Pileggi) agrees with the Sixth. Certiorari granted 26 January 2026. Set for argument 14 October 2026.

## Petitioner's core arguments (three bullets, from the merits brief)
- Plain text: §2710(a)(1) reaches "any renter, purchaser, or subscriber of goods or services from a video tape service provider" with no video-specific modifier, so under the ordinary meaning of those words a newsletter subscription from a video tape service provider qualifies; "from a video tape service provider" identifies the source of the goods, not their kind (Mohamad; Wisconsin Central).
- Meaningful variation and surplusage: Congress wrote "specific video materials or services" in the neighbouring definition of personally identifiable information, §2710(a)(3), so it knew how to impose an audiovisual limit and did not do so in §2710(a)(1) (Russello; Milner); the Sixth Circuit's reading makes the two definitions overlap completely, leaves "video" and "requested or obtained" in §2710(a)(3) with no work to do, and gives "goods or services" different meanings in §2710(a)(1) and §2710(b)(2)(D)(ii) (Pulsifer; Duncan v. Walker).
- The audiovisual limitation is a judicial addition that the title, headings, remedies and legislative history cannot supply (Brogan; Railroad Trainmen); liability attaches on disclosure of protected information, so the breadth of "consumer" only determines whose information is protected, and policy objections cannot rewrite the text; the Second and Seventh Circuits are right and the judgment should be vacated and remanded.

## Respondent's core arguments (three bullets, from the merits brief)
- Text read as a whole: "goods or services" is tied to "renter, purchaser, or subscriber" and to "from a video tape service provider," §2710(a)(1) and (a)(4), and in that setting it reaches only the audiovisual goods or services that make the provider a video tape service provider; the canons petitioner invokes (meaningful variation, surplusage, consistent usage, "any") do not carry his reading.
- Structure, history and purpose: the definition of personally identifiable information, the consent provisions (including the 2013 two-year advance-consent amendment), and the Act's title and headings all tie consumer status to video transactions; the VPPA answered the disclosure of Judge Bork's video-rental history, and the Senate Report says a department store that sells videotapes must protect only its video transactions.
- Petitioner's reading is unworkable and punitive: it would make nearly everyone who ever bought anything from a company that also sells video a "consumer," and a statute carrying liquidated and punitive damages should be construed strictly; in any event petitioner does not allege that the newsletter itself contained or linked to audiovisual content, only to articles available to anyone, so the judgment should be affirmed.
