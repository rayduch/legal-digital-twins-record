## Question(s) presented (verbatim from the Court's QP list)
Certiorari granted 1 June 2026, limited to the following question (as the Court's order states it):
1. Whether a claim regarding application of time credits under the First Step Act of 2018, 132 Stat. 5195–5208 (codified in relevant part at 18 U. S. C. §§3631–3635), seeking accelerated transfer to a halfway house or home confinement, can be brought in a habeas petition under 28 U. S. C. §2241.

(The pro se petition framed two questions: I, whether disputes over the calculation of an inmate's earned First Step Act time credits, enabling earlier transfer to halfway house or home confinement "similar to parole", are actionable under 28 U.S.C. §2241; and II, whether, given the alleged obstruction of the administrative-remedy process, exhaustion is interwoven with the underlying claims so as to create a fact issue for a jury. Only the question above, as reformulated by the Court, was granted. By order of 16 June 2026 the Court invited Brian W. Barnes to brief and argue as amicus curiae in support of the judgment below. The case was captioned Maxwell v. Thomas at the time of the grant; the acting warden, Sandra Dinis, was substituted as respondent in July 2026.)

## Lower court and holding (two sentences)
United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, 133 F.4th 453 (No. 23-40699, published). Held: a federal prisoner's claim that the Bureau of Prisons miscalculated his First Step Act earned time credits, seeking earlier transfer to a halfway house or home confinement rather than release from custody, is not cognizable in a §2241 habeas petition under the circuit's bright-line rule in Melot v. Bergami, 970 F.3d 596 (5th Cir. 2020) — unless a favourable ruling would automatically entitle the prisoner to accelerated release, the proper vehicle is a civil-rights (Bivens) action — so the district court's dismissal was affirmed on that ground without reaching exhaustion.

## Procedural posture
Petitioner is a federal prisoner at FCI Beaumont Low, Texas, serving a 20-year sentence imposed in 2014 for offences arising from a scheme to take over a mortgage-loan company. From 2020 he sought earlier transfer to home confinement or a halfway house under the First Step Act's earned-time-credit provisions (and other authorities); the warden and the Bureau of Prisons denied the requests. In February 2022 he filed a §2241 petition in the Eastern District of Texas alleging that the Bureau had failed to count some of his productive activities, which he said entitled him to 42–48 months of credits. The district court, adopting a magistrate judge's report, dismissed for failure to exhaust administrative remedies. The Fifth Circuit affirmed on the different ground, raised on its own motion, that the claim was not cognizable in habeas. Certiorari granted 1 June 2026, limited to the cognizability question. The United States, as respondent, filed a brief supporting vacatur; on 16 June 2026 the Court invited Brian W. Barnes to brief and argue as amicus curiae in support of the judgment below. The Solicitor General's motion for divided argument was granted 13 October 2026. Set for argument 2 November 2026.

**Alignment of the sides in this case.** Because the United States, the nominal respondent, agrees with petitioner that the Fifth Circuit's rule is wrong and that the judgment should not stand, the two sides in this packet are defined by the disposition sought, not by the caption: **petitioner** is the position that the Fifth Circuit's judgment should be reversed or vacated because a First Step Act time-credit claim seeking transfer to a halfway house or home confinement can be brought under §2241 (held by Maxwell, and by the United States on narrower terms); **respondent** is the position that the judgment should be affirmed because such a claim is not cognizable in habeas (held by the court-appointed amicus). A vote for petitioner is a vote to reverse or vacate; a vote for respondent is a vote to affirm.

## Petitioner's core arguments (three bullets, from the merits brief)
- Text: §2241 reaches any prisoner "in custody in violation of" federal law, and §2243 directs courts to dispose of the matter "as law and justice require", which authorises relief short of outright release; §2255(e)'s saving clause, as read in Jones v. Hendrix (2023), preserves habeas challenges to the execution of a sentence, including the denial of parole or good-time credits, and a First Step Act time-credit claim is of that kind.
- History and precedent: the common-law writ could move a prisoner to a lesser form of custody (bail), as In re Bonner, Ex parte Hull and Escoe v. Zerbst recognise; Preiser v. Rodriguez treated a claim for release from prison to parole as the core of habeas, and Wilkinson v. Dotson (including Justice Scalia's concurrence), Skinner v. Switzer and Trump v. J.G.G. (2025) confirm that habeas reaches changes in the level of custody, not only its fact or duration.
- No adequate alternative: §1983 does not reach federal officials, Bivens offers only damages, and the APA is barred by 18 U.S.C. §3625, so the Fifth and Eighth Circuit rules (Melot; Fortner v. Eischen (8th Cir. 2026)) leave a prisoner with a meritorious claim to earlier transfer into a materially less restrictive form of custody with no remedy at all; the judgment should be reversed.

## Respondent's core arguments (three bullets, from the merits briefs)
*"Respondent" here means the defence of the judgment below. The United States, though captioned as respondent, supports vacatur and is listed first for completeness; the court-appointed amicus defends the judgment.*
- **United States (supporting vacatur):** a First Step Act time-credit claim seeking transfer to a halfway house or home confinement is cognizable in habeas, because Preiser and Dotson treat release into the community as habeas relief and a parolee, too, remains in legal custody; the Fifth Circuit's suggestion that the claim belongs in a Bivens suit was "plainly wrong", though not every claim that reduces the level of custody is cognizable. But petitioner failed to exhaust, and 18 U.S.C. §3621(b) as amended by the First Step Act bars judicial review of the Bureau's designation of a place of imprisonment, including prerelease placement, consistently with the Suspension Clause; so the Court should dismiss the writ as improvidently granted (the question may be academic, with petitioner's projected halfway-house date in January 2027) or vacate and remand for the lower courts to address exhaustion and §3621(b) in the first instance.
- **Court-appointed amicus (supporting affirmance):** habeas reaches only the fact or duration of confinement, not its conditions — Preiser, Muhammad v. Close, Dotson and Skinner tie the writ to claims that would necessarily shorten detention, and the common-law history (Blackstone; Justice Scalia in Dotson) is to the same effect; transfer to a halfway house or home confinement is not "release": a person in prerelease custody under 18 U.S.C. §3624(c) remains a "prisoner" in the Bureau's custody who can be returned to prison at the Bureau's discretion, unlike a parolee or a person on supervised release (United States v. Johnson; Reno v. Koray), and Jones v. Hendrix does not help petitioner because his sentence authorises his present place of imprisonment.
- **Court-appointed amicus (continued):** both parties' proposed tests — petitioner's "materially less restrictive" custody and the government's "transfer into the community" — are unworkable, since some prison camps are less restrictive than halfway houses and the Bureau's own "community" custody category includes camps; a prisoner with a genuine claim can seek an equitable injunction against ultra vires action (Armstrong v. Exceptional Child Center), or APA or mandamus relief, and the Fifth Circuit's Bivens suggestion was mistaken only because petitioner seeks equitable rather than monetary relief; the judgment should be affirmed.
