## Question(s) presented (verbatim from the Court's QP list)
The Anti-Terrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act of 1996 (AEDPA) prohibits successive habeas applications by state prisoners with only narrow exceptions, including for claims that "rel[y] on a new rule of constitutional law, made retroactive to cases on collateral review by the Supreme Court, that was previously unavailable." 28 U.S.C. § 2244(b)(2)(A). Near-identical language allows federal prisoners to file a successive habeas motion that contains "a new rule of constitutional law, made retroactive to cases on collateral review by the Supreme Court, that was previously unavailable." Id. § 2255(h)(2). The courts of appeals are divided over whether claims rely on "a new rule ... that was previously unavailable" when a claim based on the rule could have been raised in an earlier federal habeas petition but would not have succeeded. The Eleventh and Fourth Circuits hold that whether a claim would have been meritorious does not affect whether the rule was previously available. In the decision below, the Fifth Circuit adhered to its view (and that of the Ninth Circuit) that a claim was previously available only if it had "some possibility of merit" based on the evidence available to the petitioner at the time of an earlier petition.

The question presented is: Whether a claim relies on a "a new rule of constitutional law, made retroactive to cases on collateral review by the Supreme Court, that was previously unavailable" when the habeas petitioner could have asserted a claim based on the rule in a prior federal habeas petition.

(Certiorari granted 15 June 2026 without limitation. Petitioner's merits brief restates the question as whether a claim relies on such a rule "when this Court announced the rule before the filing of the prisoner's prior federal habeas petition"; respondent restates it as whether a new, retroactive rule was "previously unavailable" under 28 U.S.C. §2244(b)(2)(A) if a claim invoking that rule had no possibility of merit when the petitioner filed his prior habeas petition.)

## Lower court and holding (two sentences)
United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, No. 23-70002 (unpublished panel opinion), rehearing en banc denied by a 9–7 vote, 164 F.4th 398, with a dissent from the denial (Judge Ho). Held: applying the circuit's rule from In re Cathey that a new rule is "previously unavailable" under §2244(b)(2)(A) when a claim invoking it earlier would have had no possibility of merit — a rule the panel described as equating legal availability with changes in the standards for psychiatric evaluation — the court affirmed the district court's denial of the State's motion to dismiss the prisoner's successive Atkins claim under §2244(b)(4).

## Procedural posture
Respondent Dexter Johnson was convicted of capital murder in Texas in 2007 for the 2006 kidnapping, rape and murder of Maria Aparece and sentenced to death; the conviction and sentence were affirmed and state habeas relief denied. His first federal habeas petition did not raise a claim under Atkins v. Virginia (2002) that he is intellectually disabled; it argued that he was mentally ill and only borderline intellectually disabled and asked that Atkins be extended to the mentally ill. The Fifth Circuit affirmed the denial of that petition in 2015 and certiorari was denied in 2016. In 2017 this Court decided Moore v. Texas, rejecting the Texas courts' Briseño factors, and in 2018 the Texas Court of Criminal Appeals adopted the DSM-5 as the governing clinical standard. Shortly before a scheduled execution in 2019 Johnson filed a state application raising an Atkins claim, which the Court of Criminal Appeals dismissed as an abuse of the writ, and then sought the Fifth Circuit's authorisation to file a successive federal application, relying on the DSM-5 (published 2013), new IQ testing and expert evidence; a panel authorised the filing under §2244(b)(2)(A). The Director moved to dismiss under §2244(b)(4); the district court (S.D. Tex., October 2022) denied the motion, considering itself bound by the circuit's futility exception, and certified the order for interlocutory appeal. The Fifth Circuit panel affirmed (23 July 2025) and rehearing en banc was denied 9–7 (12 January 2026). Certiorari granted 15 June 2026. The United States filed as amicus supporting petitioner; the Solicitor General's motion to participate in argument and for divided argument was granted 13 October 2026. Set for argument 4 November 2026. (The State is the petitioner and lost below; a vote for petitioner is a vote to reverse, a vote for respondent a vote to affirm.)

## Petitioner's core arguments (three bullets, from the merits brief)
- Text: in §2244(b)(2)(A) "previously unavailable" modifies "rule", not "claim", so availability turns on when this Court announced the rule, not on whether a claim invoking it would have succeeded; Atkins was decided in 2002, before Johnson's crime, his conviction and his first federal petition, so it was not previously unavailable (Teague v. Lane; Atkins).
- Structure: Congress separated claims resting on new law, §2244(b)(2)(A), from claims resting on new facts, §2244(b)(2)(B), and required the latter to show innocence of the offence; a claim built on the DSM-5 and new IQ evidence is a new-facts claim going only to eligibility for the death penalty, and reading subparagraph (A) to cover it would make subparagraph (B) meaningless (Jones v. Hendrix; Bowe v. United States).
- The Fifth Circuit's futility exception (In re Cathey) — asking whether the claim had "some possibility of merit" earlier — has no footing in the statute, makes "new rule" surplusage, revives the pre-AEDPA abuse-of-the-writ inquiry (McCleskey v. Zant) and defeats the finality and comity interests AEDPA protects (Banister v. Davis; Rivers v. Guerrero; Harrington v. Richter), as this claim, raised two decades after the crime on the eve of execution, illustrates; the judgment should be reversed and the successive application dismissed.

## Respondent's core arguments (three bullets, from the merits brief)
- Text: "previously unavailable" modifies "rule", but availability is practical — a rule is "available" only if it is capable of being used for its purpose (Booth v. Churner; Ross v. Blake) — so a rule under which a claim had no possibility of merit was unavailable; the Director's reading makes "previously unavailable" add nothing to "new", and the Fifth Circuit's rule is narrow in practice (Halprin rejected new evidence as a basis under (b)(2)(A); only two petitioners have been authorised under Cathey).
- Atkins alone supplies the constitutional rule, and it was unavailable to Johnson as applied: until Moore v. Texas (2017) the Texas courts applied the judge-made Briseño factors and the DSM-IV-TR IQ threshold, under which his IQ scores of 78 and 88 were a bar; the DSM-5 was published while his first federal petition was pending, when amendment was impracticable and the claim unexhausted; the claim rests on a change in the governing legal framework, not on newly discovered facts, so it belongs under (b)(2)(A), not (b)(2)(B).
- Alternatively the writ should be dismissed as improvidently granted, because the question the Director now presses differs from the question certified below and from the question in the petition; otherwise the judgment should be affirmed.
