## Question(s) presented (verbatim from the Court's QP list)
1. Whether federal law precludes state-law claims seeking relief for injuries allegedly caused by the effects of interstate and international greenhouse-gas emissions on the global climate.
2. (Directed by the Court on granting certiorari:) Whether this Court has statutory and Article III jurisdiction to hear this case.

## Lower court and holding (two sentences)
Colorado Supreme Court, in an original proceeding reviewing the trial court's refusal to dismiss (2025 WL 1363355). Held: the county's state-law tort claims against fossil-fuel producers for climate-related harms are not precluded by federal law at the pleading stage, and the case may proceed in Colorado state court.

## Procedural posture
Certiorari granted 23 February 2026 to the Colorado Supreme Court, with the parties directed to brief the additional question of this Court's jurisdiction. The ruling below is interlocutory (a denial of dismissal), so the Court must first decide whether it is a "final judgment" reviewable under 28 U.S.C. §1257(a) and whether petitioners have Article III standing; only then does it reach preemption. Set for argument 5 October 2026.

## Petitioner's core arguments (three bullets, from the merits brief)
- Jurisdiction exists: the Colorado Supreme Court's decision terminated a self-contained original proceeding and is "final" under Atlantic Richfield v. Christian (2020), or alternatively under the fourth Cox Broadcasting category; petitioners have Article III standing because the ruling rejects their federal defense, binds Colorado courts, and imposes continuing litigation costs.
- On the merits, claims about the effects of interstate and international emissions fall within an inherently federal area: the Constitution's structure (equal sovereignty; no extraterritorial state regulation) means state law cannot govern interstate air and water, the Clean Air Act replaced federal common law without authorising state law to reach interstate emissions, and suing producers rather than emitters does not change the gravamen of the claims.
- Independently, the claims intrude on the federal government's exclusive control of foreign affairs (climate diplomacy and energy policy), and the Clean Air Act preempts them of its own force.

## Respondent's core arguments (three bullets, from the merits brief)
- No jurisdiction: the decision below merely declined to dismiss on the pleadings, so any injury is speculative and litigation costs are not a cognisable Article III injury; it is not a final judgment under §1257(a), no Cox Broadcasting exception applies, and the appeal was not a writ proceeding of the Atlantic Richfield kind.
- There is no "preemption in vacuo": American Electric Power v. Connecticut (2011) and International Paper v. Ouellette (1987) recognised that the federal common law of interstate pollution was displaced by statute and treated state-law claims as an ordinary statutory-preemption question, with a presumption against preemption; those claims were never within the old federal common law anyway, since respondents are not states, seek damages not abatement, and sue producers for deceptive marketing.
- Nothing implicit in the Constitution bars a state from applying its own law to in-state harm from out-of-state conduct; interstate effects are policed by choice-of-law limits, personal jurisdiction and the dormant Commerce Clause, not by a standardless extraterritoriality principle; the Clean Air Act does not expressly or impliedly preempt state tort law.
