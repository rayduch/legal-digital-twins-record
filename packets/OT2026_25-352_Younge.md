## Question(s) presented (verbatim from the Court's QP list)
Rule 8(c)(1) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure provides that a party "must" plead "any" affirmative defense.

The question presented is: Where a defendant has filed an answer without pleading an affirmative defense, may the defendant nonetheless assert that affirmative defense as the basis for a summary judgment motion, without amending or seeking to amend its answer to plead that affirmative defense, and may a defendant do so even if an amendment adding that affirmative defense would be barred by Rule 16(b)(4)?

(Certiorari granted 30 March 2026 without limitation. Respondent restates the question as whether a district court can consider an unpleaded affirmative defense raised in a summary-judgment motion where the defendant's delay in raising the defense has caused no prejudice to the plaintiff.)

## Lower court and holding (two sentences)
United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit, No. 23-11418, 2025 WL 974309 (1 April 2025, unpublished). Held: under circuit precedent (Hassan v. U.S. Postal Service), a defendant may raise an unpleaded affirmative defense for the first time at summary judgment, without amending its answer, provided the plaintiff suffers no prejudice; applying that rule, the court held that petitioner's Title VII pregnancy-discrimination claim was barred by the "personal staff" exclusion from the statutory definition of "employee", 42 U.S.C. §2000e(f), and affirmed summary judgment for the District Attorney's Office.

## Procedural posture
Petitioner was Deputy Chief of Staff and Director of Policy and Programs in the Fulton County (Georgia) District Attorney's Office. She alleges that after she told the District Attorney on 1 July 2019 that she was pregnant he reacted angrily, stripped her of duties and dismissed her on 15 July 2019; she sued under Title VII for pregnancy discrimination. The Office answered in March 2021 without pleading the personal-staff exclusion; the scheduling order's deadline for amending pleadings passed in May 2021; the Office first raised the exclusion in March 2022 and in May 2022 moved for summary judgment on that basis without seeking leave to amend its answer. The magistrate judge and the district court (N.D. Ga., order of 29 March 2023 adopting the report and recommendation) applied the Eleventh Circuit's no-prejudice rule, found sufficient evidence of discriminatory motive to reach a jury, but entered summary judgment on the personal-staff exclusion. The Eleventh Circuit affirmed. Certiorari granted 30 March 2026. Set for argument 2 November 2026. (Petitioner lost below; a vote for petitioner is a vote to reverse, a vote for respondent a vote to affirm.)

## Petitioner's core arguments (three bullets, from the merits brief)
- Plain text: Rules 8(b)(1)(A), 8(c)(1) and 12(b) use mandatory and universal language — a party "must" state "any" avoidance or affirmative defense, and "every" defense must be asserted in the responsive pleading — which leaves no room for a judge-made exception allowing a defense to be raised for the first time in a dispositive motion; the D.C. Circuit (Harris v. Secretary, Department of Veterans Affairs) reads the Rules that way.
- The Eleventh Circuit's "no prejudice" test circumvents Rules 15 and 16: it replaces the Rule 15(a)(2) standard for amendment (undue delay, bad faith, futility, prejudice) and bypasses the Rule 16(b)(4) good-cause requirement for amendments after the scheduling-order deadline, so a defendant who would be refused leave to amend can obtain the same result by moving for summary judgment; this also creates a double standard, since the circuit requires plaintiffs to amend formally, subject to Rule 16, before pursuing an unpleaded claim (Gilmour v. Gates, McDonald & Co.), and the Tenth Circuit instead treats such a summary-judgment argument as a motion to amend.
- The practice skews briefing and divides the circuits: defendants often first address pleading in a reply, leaving plaintiffs no chance to respond (Rules 56(f)(2), 7(b)(1)(B)), and the First, Sixth and Seventh Circuits impose further prerequisites such as absence of undue delay, good faith or excuse; the judgment should be reversed.

## Respondent's core arguments (three bullets, from the merits brief)
- Text and structure: Rule 8 says a defense "must" be pleaded but does not specify waiver as the consequence of omission, whereas the Rules say so expressly where waiver is intended (Rules 12(h), 71.1(e)(3)); Rule 61 directs courts to disregard errors that do not affect substantial rights; pleadings frame factual disputes while legal arguments travel on a separate track (Johnson v. City of Shelby; Dupree v. Younger), and Rules 15 and 16 govern amendments of fact, not purely legal arguments.
- History and precedent: the Rules replaced technical pleading with a function-over-form regime descending from Equity Rule 19 and the 1919 harmless-error statute; the Court treats an inadvertent failure to plead a defense as forfeiture rather than waiver (Wood v. Milyard; Day v. McDonough) and favours substance over form (Foman v. Davis; Parrish v. United States); courts may consider purely legal arguments late in litigation, even on appeal (Singleton v. Wulff; Jones v. Bock), and a prejudice test properly confines that to arguments resolvable without disputed facts.
- Applied here, the personal-staff exclusion turned on undisputed facts and caused no prejudice, so it was properly considered (while the policymaking-appointee exclusion, which would have required new discovery, was not reached); the fears of sandbagging and skewed briefing are unfounded, there is no double standard, and the asserted circuit conflict is largely semantic; the judgment should be affirmed.
