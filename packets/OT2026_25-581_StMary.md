## Question(s) presented (verbatim from the Court's QP list)
Colorado's so-called universal preschool program pays for families to send their children to the preschool of their choice, public or private. To participate, preschools must ensure all families have an "equal opportunity" to enroll regardless of, inter alia, race, religious affiliation, sexual orientation, gender identity, income level, or disability. Colorado nonetheless permits numerous exemptions from this requirement, both categorical and discretionary, allowing preschools to admit only "children of color," "gender-nonconforming children," "the LGBTQ community," low-income families, and children with disabilities. But Colorado excludes Catholic preschools because they admit only families who support Catholic beliefs, including on sex and gender.

The Tenth Circuit upheld Colorado's decision to exclude Catholic preschools. Applying Employment Division v. Smith, it held that Colorado's secular exemptions and discretion did not undermine general applicability. In so doing, the court sided with the minority position in an entrenched and acknowledged 7-4 split over what kinds of exemptions and discretion undermine general applicability. The court also eschewed Carson v. Makin, concluding that its rule was inapplicable because Colorado's exclusion was not "on the explicit basis" of religion.

1. Whether proving a lack of general applicability under Employment Division v. Smith requires showing unfettered discretion or categorical exemptions for identical secular conduct.
2. Whether Carson v. Makin displaces the rule of Employment Division v. Smith only when the government explicitly excludes religious people and institutions.

(Certiorari granted 20 April 2026, limited to Questions 1 and 2 presented by the petition. Question 3 — whether Employment Division v. Smith should be overruled — was not granted.)

## Lower court and holding (two sentences)
United States Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit, 154 F.4th 752 (No. 24-1267). Held: Colorado's Universal Preschool Program equal-opportunity requirement is a neutral and generally applicable rule under Employment Division v. Smith — the program's income- and disability-based enrollment preferences do not undermine the requirement in a way comparable to the religious conduct at issue, and the Department had no discretion to grant individualised exemptions — and Carson v. Makin does not apply because the Catholic preschools were not excluded on the explicit basis of their religious status, so the district court's judgment rejecting the Free Exercise challenge was affirmed.

## Procedural posture
Colorado voters approved a universal preschool programme in 2020; the General Assembly codified it and created the Department of Early Childhood, which pays for fifteen hours a week of preschool for four-year-olds at the public, private or faith-based provider of the family's choice. Participating providers must give eligible children an equal opportunity to enroll regardless of listed characteristics including religious affiliation, sexual orientation, gender identity, income level and disability; the programme also recognises a set of enrollment preferences (including income-based and disability-based preferences and a catch-all for individually approved preferences), and at the outset a "congregation" preference for faith-based providers that was later narrowed and then repealed. Archdiocese of Denver preschools require enrolling families to support Catholic teaching on sex and marriage and follow archdiocesan guidance on gender identity; Colorado declined to grant a religious exemption from the equal-opportunity requirement, stating it had no authority to create one, and the parish preschools could not participate on their terms. Two parishes, the Archdiocese and two parents sued in August 2023 under the Free Exercise Clause. After a three-day bench trial the district court (D. Colo., 736 F. Supp. 3d 956) held that Carson was confined to programmes that expressly carve out religious organisations, that the income and disability preferences were not comparable secular exemptions under Tandon, and that the catch-all conferred no discretion under Fulton; it ruled for the plaintiffs only on the religious-affiliation aspect of the requirement, which it found not generally applicable because of the congregation preference, and enjoined that aspect while the preference remained; in the alternative it found that the requirement satisfies strict scrutiny. The Tenth Circuit affirmed. Certiorari granted 20 April 2026 (limited). The United States filed as amicus supporting petitioners; the Solicitor General's motion to participate in argument and for divided argument was granted 5 October 2026. Set for argument 3 November 2026. (Petitioners lost below; a vote for petitioner is a vote to reverse, a vote for respondent a vote to affirm.)

## Petitioner's core arguments (three bullets, from the merits brief)
- Carson controls: excluding otherwise eligible schools from a generally available benefit because of their religious exercise triggers strict scrutiny under Trinity Lutheran, Espinoza and Carson, and the Tenth Circuit's requirement that the exclusion be made on the explicit basis of religion conflicts with those cases, with Sherbert and Thomas, and with Mahmoud v. Taylor (2025); Smith is in any event a residual rule that the Court has applied against a religious claimant only twice since 1990, and it does not govern conditions on public benefits.
- Even under Smith the requirement is not generally applicable: Colorado permits income- and disability-based preferences that undermine equal access "in a similar way" to the religious preference it forbids (Lukumi; Fulton; Tandon), over a thousand of some two thousand participating preschools impose at least one preference, and the catch-all preference — which the Department's own director testified could permit preferences for "gender-nonconforming children", "children of color" and the LGBTQ community — is a mechanism for individualised exemptions that triggers strict scrutiny (Fulton; Kennedy v. Bremerton).
- Strict scrutiny is not met: the asserted interest is not compelling as applied, given the State's own exceptions and the absence of any documented harm, excluding Catholic preschools reduces rather than expands access to universal preschool, and Colorado has not shown narrow tailoring (Fulton; Espinoza; Gonzales v. O Centro); the judgment should be reversed.

## Respondent's core arguments (three bullets, from the merits brief)
- Carson, Trinity Lutheran and Espinoza apply strict scrutiny only where government singles out religious status or religious use for disfavoured treatment; Maine's programme in Carson excluded religious schools as such, whereas UPK includes religious providers and religious instruction and asks of them only what it asks of every provider; petitioners' proposed rule for conditions on public benefits has no limiting principle and would reach ordinary programme conditions of every kind, and Fulton declined to adopt such a categorical rule.
- The equal-opportunity requirement is neutral and generally applicable under Smith, Lukumi, Fulton and Tandon: it applies to all providers, religious and secular, and regulates only whether a family may be denied enrollment on protected grounds; the specialised-services preferences for children with disabilities and low-income children operate within the requirement rather than as exceptions to it, and the Department has no statutory authority to grant individualised exemptions, so there is no discretionary mechanism of the Fulton kind.
- The trial record forecloses petitioners' account: after a three-day trial the district court found no evidence of non-neutrality or hostility and found that the Department lacked discretion to grant exemptions, findings the Tenth Circuit affirmed; the congregation preference found unlawful was removed, and the first-year programmatic adjustments did not depart from the equal-opportunity requirement; in any event the requirement serves a compelling interest in ensuring that children are not denied publicly funded preschool on the basis of protected traits and is narrowly tailored to it; the judgment should be affirmed.
