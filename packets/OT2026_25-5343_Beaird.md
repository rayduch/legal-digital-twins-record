## Lower court and holding (two sentences)
United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit, No. 24-10764, 2025 WL 1410410 (unpublished). Held: the sentencing enhancement under U.S.S.G. §2K2.1(a)(3) for a semiautomatic firearm "capable of accepting a large capacity magazine" was properly applied under the Guidelines commentary (Application Note 2, defining the term as a magazine holding more than 15 rounds), because circuit precedent — United States v. Vargas (en banc), holding that Stinson still governs after Kisor and that commentary controls even where the guideline is unambiguous, and United States v. Martin, treating the magazine definition as authoritative — foreclosed the argument.

## Procedural posture
Petitioner pleaded guilty in the Northern District of Texas to being a felon in possession of a firearm, 18 U.S.C. §922(g)(1); the firearm was a Glock 17 with its standard 17-round magazine. The district court, considering itself bound by Application Note 2, applied the §2K2.1(a)(3) enhancement, which raised the Guidelines range from 51–63 to 63–78 months, and imposed 72 months; it stated for the record that it would have imposed the same sentence regardless of the range. The Fifth Circuit affirmed on the basis of circuit precedent. Certiorari granted 20 April 2026, limited to the Stinson question. The United States, as respondent, filed a brief supporting vacatur; on 6 May 2026 the Court invited Anthony J. Dick to brief and argue as amicus curiae in support of the judgment below. The Solicitor General's motion for divided argument was granted 1 October 2026. Set for argument 13 October 2026.

**Alignment of the sides in this case.** Because the United States, the nominal respondent, agrees with petitioner that the judgment should be vacated, the two sides in this packet are defined by the disposition sought, not by the caption: **petitioner** is the position that the Fifth Circuit's judgment should be vacated (held by Beaird and by the United States, on different reasoning); **respondent** is the position that the judgment should be affirmed because Stinson still states the rule (held by the court-appointed amicus). ## Petitioner's core arguments (three bullets, from the merits brief)
- Courts should give no deference to Guidelines commentary: the Sentencing Reform Act assigns the interpretation of the Guidelines to courts, commentary bypasses the Act's notice-and-comment and congressional-review safeguards, and deference in criminal sentencing raises separation-of-powers and lenity concerns; after Loper Bright, courts exercise independent judgment.
- Stinson cannot survive Kisor: Stinson's "plainly erroneous or inconsistent" test is the reflexive Seminole Rock/Auer deference Kisor disavowed, its underpinnings have eroded, and the agency-deference rationale does not fit criminal sentencing; stare decisis does not bar revisiting a rule the Court's later decisions have already undermined, and the Commission can move any rule it wants to keep into Guidelines text.
- In the alternative, Kisor's limits apply in full: courts must exhaust the traditional tools of construction, including lenity, before deferring, so deference should rarely if ever apply to commentary that increases a sentence; the judgment should be vacated and the case remanded for the Fifth Circuit to interpret §2K2.1(a)(3) without deference to the commentary.

## Respondent's core arguments (three bullets, from the merits briefs)
*"Respondent" here means the defence of the judgment below. The United States, though captioned as respondent, supports vacatur and is listed first for completeness; the court-appointed amicus defends the judgment.*
- **United States (supporting vacatur):** Stinson remains correct — commentary is analogous to an agency's interpretation of its own legislative rule, an analogy the Congressional Review Act strengthened — but it operates within Kisor's limits: deference only where the guideline is genuinely ambiguous, the commentary's reading is reasonable, and its character and context warrant it; the Fifth Circuit's rule that commentary controls even unambiguous guidelines is a caricature of the doctrine, so the judgment should be vacated and remanded for the lower courts to apply Kisor, address harmlessness (Molina-Martinez) and revisit circuit precedent, this Court being one of review, not first view.
- **Court-appointed amicus (supporting affirmance):** Stinson still states the rule — commentary is authoritative unless inconsistent with, or a plainly erroneous reading of, the guideline — because commentary is an exercise of the Commission's express statutory authority to issue policy statements (28 U.S.C. §994(a)(2); Williams v. United States), not an implied delegation, so Kisor's limits do not apply and importing Kisor's ambiguity trigger would conflict with Loper Bright; and after Booker the advisory Guidelines do not bind private conduct, so the fair-notice, lenity and separation-of-powers objections to agency deference largely fall away (Beckles).
- **Court-appointed amicus (continued):** Stinson is a statutory precedent with enhanced stare decisis force; Congress has reviewed commentary for decades without curtailing it; the rule is workable and reliance on it pervades the Manual, so the disruption to sentencing from overruling weighs heavily against doing so; and in any event petitioner does not dispute that his magazine falls within Application Note 2 and the district court said it would have imposed the same sentence, so the question may not change the result here.
