# Case packet — Anderson v. Intel Corp. Investment Policy Committee, No. 25-498

## Question(s) presented (verbatim from the Court's QP list)
1. Under the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974 (ERISA), a plan fiduciary is subject to a "[p]rudent man standard of care," which requires the fiduciary to "discharge his duties with respect to a plan" with the "care, skill, prudence, and diligence" that a prudent person "acting in a like capacity and familiar with such matters would use." 29 U.S.C. § 1104(a)(1). As this Court has recognized, a court's inquiry into whether a plaintiff has adequately alleged that a fiduciary breached ERISA's duty of prudence "will necessarily be context specific" because the content of that duty "turns on 'the circumstances ... prevailing' at the time the fiduciary acts." Fifth Third Bancorp. v. Dudenhoeffer, 573 U.S. 409, 425 (2014) (quoting 29 U.S.C. § 1104(a)(1)(B)). As a result, "categorical" pleading rules are "inconsistent with the context-specific inquiry that ERISA requires." Hughes v. Nw. Univ., 595 U.S. 170, 173 (2022). The question presented is: Whether, for claims predicated on fund underperformance, pleading that an ERISA fiduciary failed to use the requisite "care, skill, prudence, or diligence" under the circumstances and thus breached ERISA's duty of prudence when investing plan assets requires alleging a "meaningful benchmark."

## Lower court and holding (two sentences)
United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, 137 F.4th 1015 (Berzon, J., concurring). Held: the participants' duty-of-prudence claims against Intel's plan fiduciaries, predicated on the underperformance of custom target-date and diversified funds with large alternative-asset allocations, were properly dismissed because the complaint did not identify a "meaningful benchmark" — a comparator with similar objectives, risks and potential rewards — and the pleaded comparators (largely passive, equity-heavy retail funds) had different aims and risk profiles.

## Procedural posture
Certiorari granted 16 January 2026 to the Ninth Circuit, which affirmed the district court's dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6). The question is the pleading standard, not the sufficiency of these particular comparators; respondents argue the latter was not granted. Set for argument 6 October 2026.

## Petitioner's core arguments (three bullets, from the merits brief)
- Dudenhoeffer and Hughes forbid bright-line pleading rules for duty-of-prudence claims; the inquiry is context-specific and governed by Twombly/Iqbal read on "the allegations as a whole", and employees typically lack the inside information a categorical rule would demand.
- The Ninth Circuit's meaningful-benchmark rule (borrowed from the Eighth Circuit) contradicts the statutory text, the Federal Rules and this Court's precedent; it is over-inclusive, screens out relevant non-comparative allegations, effectively immunises reckless strategies no other fiduciary follows, and requires courts to decide "how close is close enough" as a matter of law at the pleading stage.
- The ordinary plausibility standard already filters weak complaints; the judgment should be vacated for an assessment of whether all the allegations taken together plausibly state a claim.

## Respondent's core arguments (three bullets, from the merits brief)
- ERISA's text and the trust-law it draws on allow fiduciaries a wide range of goals and risk tolerances and judge them prospectively by process, not hindsight; an allegation that a fund underperformed some alternative implies nothing about imprudent process unless the comparator is similarly situated — as the statute's "like capacity … like aims" language itself requires.
- The meaningful-benchmark requirement is not a heightened standard but an application of Twombly/Iqbal to a specific kind of allegation, exactly as Dudenhoeffer crafted categorical rules for stock-drop claims; a "meaningless" comparator provides no plausible inference, and the Ninth Circuit did not require a comparator for every theory, only for claims predicated on underperformance.
- Petitioners' approach would punish risk-mitigation strategies Congress sought to encourage, drive up plan costs and force settlements of meritless suits given discovery costs; the pleaded comparators here were plainly dissimilar and the fact-bound question of their adequacy was not granted.
