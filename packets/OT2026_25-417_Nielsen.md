## Question(s) presented (verbatim from the Court's QP list)
1. Whether the Ninth Circuit here erred in recognizing a Bivens cause of action.

(The Court's QP page precedes the question with the petition's framing: "This case concerns the judicially created damages remedy first recognized in Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics, 403 U.S. 388 (1971). In Carlson v. Green, 446 U.S. 14 (1980), the Court extended Bivens to an Eighth Amendment claim of deliberate indifference to medical needs where prison officials failed to address an inmate's acute asthma attack and exacerbated it, causing his death on-site within hours. Since then, this Court has emphasized that the creation of damages actions rests with Congress, not courts. The judicially created Bivens remedy thus cannot be extended to any 'new Bivens context' if any 'special factor counsel[s] hesitation' in doing so. Ziglar v. Abbasi, 582 U.S. 120, 139-140 (2017). '[T]he new-context inquiry is easily satisfied,' encompassing cases with 'meaningful[ly]' different facts, or 'potential special factors'—including 'alternative remedial structures'—'not consider[ed]' in the Court's prior cases. Egbert v. Boule, 596 U.S. 482, 492-493 (2022). '[A] court may not recognize a Bivens remedy' if 'there is any reason to think that Congress might be better equipped to create a damages remedy.' Id. at 492. In this case, respondent alleged that he suffered a fractured coccyx in a prison gang fight, causing chronic pain, but was not sent to a hospital. The Ninth Circuit held the suit did not present a 'new' context from Carlson—notwithstanding an alternative remedial scheme this Court did not consider in Carlson, and despite significant factual differences such as the immediacy and severity of the harm alleged." Certiorari granted 22 June 2026 without limitation.)

## Lower court and holding (two sentences)
United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, 115 F.4th 1034 (No. 23-15605; Paez and Koh, JJ.; M. Smith, J., dissenting), rehearing en banc denied over the dissent of eleven judges, 139 F.4th 1056. Held: a federal prisoner's Eighth Amendment claim that a prison medical officer was deliberately indifferent to a serious medical need (a fractured coccyx left untreated beyond over-the-counter pain relief) does not present a new Bivens context but falls within Carlson v. Green, so the court reversed the district court's dismissal without reaching the special-factors step.

## Procedural posture
Respondent Kekai Watanabe, then a federal inmate at the Federal Detention Center in Honolulu, alleges that after a gang assault in 2021 he reported severe back pain to petitioner Francis Nielsen, a paramedic on the facility's medical staff, who treated him but refused his requests to be taken to a hospital; he was given over-the-counter pain medication and was later diagnosed with a fractured coccyx. He sued Nielsen for damages under Bivens for deliberate indifference to serious medical needs. The District of Hawaii dismissed, holding that the claim arose in a new context and that special factors — including the Bureau of Prisons' Administrative Remedy Program — foreclosed a Bivens remedy. The Ninth Circuit reversed. Certiorari granted 22 June 2026. The United States filed as amicus supporting petitioner; the Solicitor General's motion to participate in argument and for divided argument was granted 13 October 2026. Set for argument 9 November 2026. (Petitioner lost below; a vote for petitioner is a vote to reverse, a vote for respondent a vote to affirm.)

## Petitioner's core arguments (three bullets, from the merits brief)
- Under the two-step framework of Ziglar v. Abbasi, Hernandez v. Mesa and Egbert v. Boule, a context is "new" whenever it differs in any meaningful way from Bivens, Davis or Carlson, and a court may not recognise a remedy if there is any reason to think Congress is better equipped to create one; this case differs from Carlson, which involved a fatal medical emergency for which no non-monetary relief was possible, because the injury here was non-fatal and the prisoner could have sought administrative and injunctive relief (Correctional Services Corp. v. Malesko confirms that contexts differ even where the constitutional right and the mechanism of injury match).
- The Administrative Remedy Program, the Prison Litigation Reform Act and later legislation are alternative remedial structures and special factors that Carlson never considered; under Abbasi and Egbert the existence of such a scheme is itself a reason to treat the context as new and to leave the remedy to Congress, and the Ninth Circuit erred by pushing those factors to the second step.
- Bivens should in any event be overruled: courts lack authority to create damages remedies, the Court has repudiated Bivens's reasoning (Egbert; Hernandez), and respondent accepts that special factors defeat his claim if the context is new; the judgment should be reversed.

## Respondent's core arguments (three bullets, from the merits brief)
- The claim is Carlson's: a federal prisoner, in a federal prison, alleging Eighth Amendment deliberate indifference to a serious medical need; the difference between an injured survivor and a decedent is not a meaningful one, since Carlson's survivorship holding presupposed that the prisoner himself had a claim, and the PLRA's exhaustion and physical-injury rules presuppose living plaintiffs bringing exactly such claims.
- The Administrative Remedy Program dates from 1974 and predates Carlson; Carlson already rejected the FTCA as an adequate substitute for an individual damages claim, and the ARP shares those shortcomings; the PLRA treats the ARP as a prerequisite to a Carlson claim, not a bar, and so assumes such claims exist — neither is a "new" special factor.
- Petitioner's approach would reduce Carlson to a null set and overrule it in substance; Congress has ratified Carlson through the Westfall Act's exception for constitutional claims, 28 U.S.C. §2679(b)(2)(A), and through the PLRA; stare decisis protects a decision that rests on a federal enclave and centuries of jailer liability, is workable and has generated strong reliance; the judgment should be affirmed. (The United States, as amicus for petitioner, asks the Court to declare Carlson obsolete, since every future prisoner damages claim would arise in a new context, while leaving Bivens itself for a later case.)
