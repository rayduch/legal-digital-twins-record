## Question(s) presented (verbatim from the Court's QP list)
1. In private condemnations under the Natural Gas Act, should just compensation be determined by reference to state law?

(The Court's QP page precedes the question with the petition's framing: "The Natural Gas Act authorizes private companies to condemn land in order to build certain natural gas infrastructure, but it says nothing about how to determine the amount of just compensation owed for the property taken. 15 U.S.C. § 717f. The Third, Fifth, Sixth, and Eleventh Circuits have all held that compensation awards in private condemnations under the Natural Gas Act must therefore be determined by reference to state law, which often mandates higher compensation than the floor set by the Fifth Amendment. In this case, the Eighth Circuit expressly split with its sister circuits and instead held that the Natural Gas Act permits only the constitutional minimum of compensation required by the Fifth Amendment." Certiorari granted 29 June 2026 without limitation, after the Solicitor General's views were invited on 8 December 2025.)

## Lower court and holding (two sentences)
United States Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit, 132 F.4th 1058 (No. 24-1693). Held: when a natural-gas company exercises the federal eminent-domain power delegated by §7(h) of the Natural Gas Act, 15 U.S.C. §717f(h), the measure of compensation is the Fifth Amendment's "just compensation" standard rather than state law, so the landowners could not recover the attorney's fees and expenses that North Dakota law treats as part of compensation, and the district court's award of $383,375.76 in fees and expenses was vacated.

## Procedural posture
Respondent WBI Energy Transmission holds a FERC certificate for a roughly twelve-mile pipeline and related expansion in McKenzie County, North Dakota. Having bought most of the easements it needed, it condemned an easement across petitioners' long-held ranchland (the Hoffmanns as trustees, Denae Hoffmann, Rocky & Jonilla Farms, LLP, and Randall Stevenson) under §7(h) of the Natural Gas Act in the District of North Dakota (No. 1:18-cv-078). The parties settled the value of the interests taken, leaving the question whether petitioners could also recover attorney's fees and litigation expenses, which North Dakota law treats as part of just compensation and federal law generally leaves on the condemnee. The district court held that the Act's silence on the measure of compensation is a gap to be filled, under United States v. Kimbell Foods, with state law, and awarded $383,375.76. The Eighth Circuit reversed, expressly parting from the Third, Fifth, Sixth and Eleventh Circuits. Certiorari granted 29 June 2026. The United States filed as amicus supporting respondent; the Solicitor General's motion to participate in argument and for divided argument was granted 13 October 2026. Set for argument 9 November 2026. (Petitioners lost below; a vote for petitioner is a vote to reverse, a vote for respondent a vote to affirm.)

## Petitioner's core arguments (three bullets, from the merits brief)
- Text and common law favour state law: §717f(h) is silent on compensation and authorises only an ordinary condemnation suit, and the background principles against which it was written — state control of property law and the narrow construction of private delegations of eminent domain (United States v. Carmack) — mean that silence leaves state law in place; under the approach Justice Scalia described in Agency Holding Corp. v. Malley-Duff, state law applies unless a federal statute displaces it, and Reconstruction Finance Corp. v. Beaver County and Brown v. United States show the Court using state property rules to fill federal silence.
- Structure, history and contemporaneous practice point the same way: §717f(h) copied the Federal Power Act's language, which the lower courts had read to incorporate state compensation law; courts of the 1947 era applied state law in these condemnations; and when Congress defined just compensation for electric transmission rights-of-way in 2005 (16 U.S.C. §824p(f)) it legislated against that understanding, which under Monessen implies acquiescence.
- Neither Kimbell Foods nor PennEast changes the result: applying state compensation law would not hinder the pipeline programme, conflict with its objectives or upset landowners' expectations (four circuits have applied it for years), and PennEast decided sovereign immunity and the power to sue, not the substantive law of compensation; the judgment should be reversed.

## Respondent's core arguments (three bullets, from the merits brief)
- Congress delegated the federal eminent-domain power itself, categorically (PennEast), and the delegatee takes that power with its incidents, including the Fifth Amendment's fair-market-value measure; Kohl v. United States holds that the federal power is complete in itself and cannot be enlarged or diminished by a State.
- United States v. Miller holds that a statute adopting only state forms and procedure does not displace the federal measure of compensation, and §717f(h) adopts state law for practice and procedure alone; by contrast the General Bridge Act of 1946 expressly tied compensation to state law, so Congress knew how to make that choice and did not make it here; a single national standard serves the Act's purpose of replacing inconsistent state regimes.
- Kimbell Foods has no role where ordinary statutory construction answers the question, and even under it a uniform federal rule would prevail; attorney's fees are not part of just compensation (United States v. Bodcaw Co.), the American Rule bars fee-shifting without express statutory authority, and Congress's separate fee provisions for condemnations (the Uniform Relocation Assistance Act) confirm that §717f(h) contains none; the judgment should be affirmed. (The United States, as amicus for respondent, adds that when Congress delegates the federal eminent-domain power without expressly adopting state law, silence means the constitutional standard applies — United States v. 93.970 Acres of Land.)
