## Question(s) presented (verbatim from the Court's QP list)
The Resource Conservation and Recovery Act of 1976 (RCRA), 42 U.S.C. 6901 et seq., is a comprehensive environmental statute providing for cradle-to-grave regulation of hazardous waste through a scheme of cooperative federalism. Under RCRA, the United States Environmental Protection Agency may authorize state and territorial regulators to administer permitting programs for hazardous-waste-treatment facilities in their respective jurisdictions, including facilities operated by the federal government. The questions presented are as follows:
1. Whether the federal government's submission to a state or territorial regulator of an application to renew a RCRA permit is "final agency action" that is immediately reviewable under the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. 704.
2. Whether the federal government must comply with the general environmental-review procedures of the National Environmental Policy Act of 1969, 42 U.S.C. 4321 et seq., before submitting a permit-renewal application under RCRA, which sets forth its own specific procedures to review environmental impacts in the context of hazardous-waste treatment.

## Lower court and holding (two sentences)
United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, 128 F.4th 1089. Held: the Air Force's 2021 application to Guam EPA to renew its RCRA permit for open burning/open detonation of munitions at Tarague Beach was final agency action reviewable under the APA, and the Air Force was required to conduct NEPA review before deciding to continue those operations, because RCRA's permitting process is not an adequate substitute for NEPA's earlier and broader analysis of alternatives.

## Procedural posture
Certiorari granted 9 March 2026 on the petition of the United States. Respondent, a Guam community organisation (Prutehi Guahan), sued under the APA; the district court dismissed and the Ninth Circuit reversed. The Court reviews both the finality holding and the NEPA-displacement holding. Set for argument 7 October 2026.

## Petitioner's core arguments (three bullets, from the merits brief)
- Under Bennett v. Spear, the renewal application is not final: it opens an iterative process with Guam EPA (including public comment) in which the application may be modified, so it is no more final than a notice of proposed rulemaking, and it determines no rights or obligations but asks the regulator to determine them; the only immediate effect, automatic extension of the existing permit, flows from the prior permit and the APA's own tolling rule, not from the application.
- RCRA's specific, comprehensive procedural and substantive requirements for hazardous-waste permits displace NEPA's general procedures (the specific governs the general); RCRA requires environmental analysis and public engagement before any permit issues, so the regulator's inquiry obviates a duplicative NEPA process by the applicant agency.
- Longstanding EPA regulations exempt RCRA permitting from NEPA and Congress has repeatedly amended RCRA without disturbing them; nothing in the statute distinguishes EPA as permitting agency from a federal applicant like the Air Force.

## Respondent's core arguments (three bullets, from the merits brief)
- NEPA's own text (42 U.S.C. §4336(a)(2)–(3)) allows displacement only where another statute says so or "clearly and fundamentally conflict[s]" with NEPA; Congress has never exempted RCRA, the two processes are complementary, and petitioners' "functional equivalence" theory contravenes the text and the strong presumption against implied repeal, and has never been applied to a non-regulator agency.
- Even under a functional-equivalence test, RCRA permitting does not fulfil NEPA's hard-look requirement, which operates at a different time and requires evaluation of different considerations, including alternative locations for open detonation.
- The Air Force's decision to continue detonation beyond 2021, memorialised in the renewal application, satisfies Bennett: the agency had made up its mind and implemented the decision on the ground, the "mere possibility" of a later change does not defeat finality (Sackett; Biden v. Texas), and the application carried concrete legal consequences by extending the permit for five years and counting.

## Doctrinal area / key precedents named in the QP or briefs
Administrative law — APA finality (Bennett v. Spear; Sackett v. EPA; Biden v. Texas); NEPA displacement by more specific statutes (Flint Ridge; Epic Systems on implied repeal); RCRA cooperative federalism.
